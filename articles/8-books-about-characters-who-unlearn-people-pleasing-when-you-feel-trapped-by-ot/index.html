If you grew up believing that keeping the peace meant keeping your own mouth shut, you probably know the exhaustion of living for everyone else’s approval. I spent years saying yes to favors I hated, biting my tongue to avoid awkwardness, and feeling completely paralyzed by what my family, boss, and friends expected of me.

Reading fiction was my accidental escape hatch. Watching well-drawn characters systematically wreck their own lives to please others—and then figure out how to stop—gave me a roadmap for my own life. If you are tired of carrying everyone else’s emotional baggage, these eight books offer messy, realistic, and deeply comforting stories about breaking the cycle of people-pleasing.

My Year of Rest and Relaxation by Ottessa Moshfegh

This book is not a gentle, cozy guide to mindfulness; it is a dark, biting look at what happens when a character completely checks out from societal expectations. The unnamed narrator has a prestigious job, an annoying best friend, and an indifferent boyfriend—and she decides to drug herself into a year-long hibernation to escape the exhausting demands of being a functional, agreeable young woman in New York City.

Why it helps: It is a masterclass in realizing that you do not owe the world your constant participation. Watching the narrator ruthlessly drop social obligations and ignore everyone’s demands is a strangely therapeutic exercise in radical disengagement.

Eleanor Oliphant Is Completely Fine by Gail Honeyman

Eleanor is rigidly polite, deeply defensive, and entirely dependent on a carefully constructed routine designed to keep people at a safe distance. She wears the same clothes, eats the exact same lunch, and avoids emotional vulnerability at all costs. But her rigid armor is really a trauma response to a childhood where she learned that anticipating everyone else’s moods was a matter of survival.

Why it helps: Eleanor’s journey shows how people-pleasing often masquerades as hyper-competence and perfectionism. Watching her slowly learn to drop her guard, accept help, and stop performing normalcy is a powerful reminder that you don’t have to be palatable to be worthy of love.

Convenience Store Woman by Sayaka Murata

Keiko has spent her entire life disappointing people without meaning to. Her family worries about her, her friends try to set her up, and society tells her that working part-time in a convenience store in her thirties is a failure. But Keiko loves the predictable, orderly world of the store, where every interaction has a script and she doesn’t have to fake emotional resonance.

Why it helps: This short, sharp novel validates anyone who has ever felt broken simply because they don’t want the life society ordered off the menu for them. Keiko’s quiet, unapologetic acceptance of her own weirdness is an antidote to the pressure to conform.

Yellowface by R.F. Kuang

June Hayward is a textbook people-pleaser who cannot handle the fact that her career hasn’t taken off the way she thinks it should. When she steals her late rival’s unpublished manuscript and publishes it as her own, she sets off a spiral of internet panic and performative anxiety. June is constantly trying to manage the public perception of her, agonizing over every tweet, review, and social interaction.

Why it helps: While this is a satirical thriller rather than a self-help story, June’s internal monologue perfectly captures the psychological prison of living entirely for external validation. It lays bare the sheer terror of being disliked and exposes how hollow “success” feels when you achieved it by bending over backward for an audience.

Book Lovers by Emily Henry

Nora Stephens is the quintessential corporate workaholic who dates men who ultimately leave her for small-town women who bake bread. She has built her entire identity around being the reliable sister, the tireless literary agent, and the person who never asks for too much. When she gets dragged to a picturesque mountain town, her carefully curated armor starts to crack.

Why it helps: Unlike many romance heroines who start out messy, Nora starts out entirely defined by her utility to others. Watching her realize that she is allowed to take up space, ask for what she needs, and stop managing her sister’s life is deeply satisfying for anyone who feels like an emotional support animal for their family.

Severance by Ling Ma

Candace is an office drone in New York who describes herself as a passive person who defaults to following instructions. Even as a fungal infection causes the literal end of the world, she keeps going to work, managing supply chains, and posting photos to her lifestyle blog because she doesn’t know how to exist outside of routine and external validation.

Why it helps: This satirical sci-fi novel brilliantly skewers the corporate work ethic and the way we internalize institutional expectations. Candace’s journey out of the city and away from her desk job is a surreal exploration of how hard it is to figure out who you actually are when you strip away the job titles and social scripts.

The Midnight Library by Matt Haig

Nora Seed finds herself in a library between life and death where every book gives her the chance to try out a life where she made different choices. She gets to experience the alternate realities where she didn’t quit swimming, didn’t leave her fiancé, and didn’t give up her rock band—all lives she avoided out of fear of disappointing others or failing to meet expectations.

Why it helps: It tackles the “what ifs” that plague chronic people-pleasers. By walking through all the versions of herself that she thought she should have been, Nora finally learns that the only expectation worth meeting is her own desire to simply exist and find peace in the present.

Nothing to See Here by Kevin Wilson

Madison is drifting through life, working dead-end jobs, and letting her wealthy, demanding friend Tennessee dictate the terms of her existence because she doesn’t know how to say no. When Tennessee asks her to take care of her stepchildren—who literally burst into flames when they get agitated—Madison finally has to step up, set hard boundaries, and protect these kids from a family that wants to exploit them.

Why it helps: Sometimes it takes an utterly bizarre crisis to shock you out of your compliance. Madison learns to set boundaries not because she suddenly grew a spine for her own sake, but because protecting others forced her to find her voice. It is a hilarious, chaotic reminder that you can start small when learning to push back.

If you are ready to stop managing everyone else’s comfort at the expense of your own mental health, picking up one of these titles is a great way to start practicing boundaries from the safety of the couch.
